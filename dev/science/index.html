Scientific Context

The results computed by CurvatureDistinguishability.jl rest on the geometric formulation, the discernibility criterion and the modelling assumptions set out below.

1. Geometry of source confusion

Consider a data stream that contains two distinct, closely overlapping gravitational-wave (GW) signals with a small parameter separation $\delta$ (the two-source hypothesis) and is fitted with a single-source model. The linear Fisher-matrix approximation predicts that the residual power, the squared distance $D^2$ between the true two-source data and the best-fit single-source manifold, scales quadratically as $\delta^2$.

The manifold of single-source waveforms $\mathcal{M}_1$ adapts to the data. The optimizer adjusts the parameters of the single source and so absorbs the linear ($\mathcal{O}(\delta)$) and quadratic ($\mathcal{O}(\delta^2)$) differences, assuming equal amplitudes.

The unabsorbable residual lies in the normal space, orthogonal to the tangent space of the signal manifold. The magnitude of this orthogonal projection is set by the extrinsic curvature $K(u)$ of the manifold along the direction $u$ of the parameter separation, and the distance $D^2$ scales quartically:

\[D^2 \approx \frac{1}{16} K(u) \delta^4\]

The signal amplitude enters $K(u)$ through the noise-weighted inner product, so no separate amplitude factor appears in the law.

2. Discernibility limit

The criterion is a residual signal-to-noise ratio: two sources count as distinguishable from a single one when the unabsorbable residual power exceeds a threshold $\rho_{\mathrm{threshold}}$,

\[D^2 \ge \rho_{\mathrm{threshold}}^2\]

Substituting the geometric scaling law gives the analytic limit of discernibility, which is the boundary of the zone of confusion:

\[\delta_{\mathrm{min}}(u) = \left( \frac{16 \cdot \rho_{\mathrm{threshold}}^2}{K(u)} \right)^{1/4}\]

Because of the $1/4$ exponent, lowering $\rho_{\mathrm{threshold}}$ yields strongly diminishing returns for resolving overlapping sources. The shipped $\rho_{\mathrm{threshold}} = 1$ is the leading-order resolvability scale, not a detection-significance threshold. With the likelihood identity $\Delta\ln\mathcal{L}_{\max} = D^2/2$ it corresponds to $\Delta\ln\mathcal{L} = 1/2$, and a Bayesian model-selection statement would in addition carry the Occam penalty of the six extra parameters of the two-source hypothesis.

3. Physical model

The pipeline implements a six-parameter frequency-domain inspiral of an aligned-spin massive black-hole binary, with TaylorF2 phasing to 1.5PN order, 0.5PN harmonic amplitudes and an innermost-stable-orbit window, observed through the long-wavelength response of the LISA constellation on its analytic orbits. The model is documented equation by equation in Waveform Physics. The state vector theta holds, in order, the luminosity distance $D_L$, the chirp mass $\mathcal{M}$, the coalescence time $t_c$, the coalescence phase $\phi_c$ and the aligned spins $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$.

The phasing carries the complete 1.5PN spin–orbit term of Poisson and Will, which couples the spins to the chirp mass and the coalescence time through the single coefficient $\beta$. The spin combination with $\mathrm{d}\beta = 0$ is therefore an exact flat direction at every mass ratio.

The $(1,1)$ and $(3,3)$ harmonics are physical: they enter with their 0.5PN amplitudes, proportional to the mass asymmetry $\sqrt{1 - 4\eta}$, and break the distance–mass–time degeneracies of a single-harmonic model without any phenomenological factor.

4. Detector response

Detector.jl projects the source-frame strain into the A and E channel combinations of the constellation, which are noise-orthogonal for equal arms and equal link noise. The projection uses long-wavelength Michelson antenna patterns on the analytic spacecraft orbits rather than a time-delay interferometry simulation, and includes the orbital Doppler phase and the finite-arm roll-off. The response is evaluated per frequency bin and per harmonic at the stationary-phase emission time. The full detector model is documented in Waveform Physics.

5. Physical bounds and the capped zone of confusion

The local differential geometry is blind to global parameter bounds, and along quasi-degenerate directions the mathematical boundary legitimately diverges. The 1.5PN phase sees the spins only through $\beta$, so along the spin combination with $\mathrm{d}\beta = 0$ ($\chi_a$ at equal mass, a tilted line otherwise) the manifold is exactly flat and $\delta_{\mathrm{min}} \to \infty$. The physical parameters are bounded nonetheless: $|\chi| \le 1$, distances, masses and times are non-negative, and phase deviations live on $[-\pi, \pi]$.

For each direction $\varphi$ the pipeline computes both the mathematical radius $r_{\mathrm{math}} = (16\rho^2/K_{\mathrm{raw}})^{1/4}$ and the distance $r_{\mathrm{box}}$ to the physical prior box. Equal source amplitudes are assumed throughout the mapping stage. The published boundary is the capped radius $\min(r_{\mathrm{math}}, r_{\mathrm{box}})$, the exact intersection of the mathematical zone with the prior. Directions where the prior takes over are flagged (Prior_Limited) and drawn distinctly in the figures. Both radii are persisted, so the raw mathematical zone remains fully recoverable from the data.

6. Computational design

Resolving distances down to $D^2 \sim 10^{-20}$ without precision loss rests on $\mathcal{O}(1)$-rescaled parameters, fused nested-dual automatic differentiation, mirrored and adaptively refined angular mapping with exact corner vertices, and box-constrained interior-point Newton optimization inside the physical priors. The implementation is documented in Architecture.
